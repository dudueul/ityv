# US Federal Law, Litigation, and Empirical Evidence on Public Access to Voter Registration Lists (as of 12 November 2026)

Scope note: this file covers the federal layer above the 50 state rules (NVRA, HAVA, DPPA, Privacy Act, SSN limits), the court cases, the 2025–2026 Department of Justice (DOJ) voter-roll campaign, and the evidence on benefits and harms. State-by-state rules are covered elsewhere. Dates are given for every item. Where I could only read a search-engine summary or a secondary report rather than the primary document, I say so.

## Q1. NVRA Section 8(i), 52 U.S.C. § 20507(i): what it requires, how courts read it, whether it forces release of the full list, and whether states may restrict uses

### Takeaway
Section 8(i) requires states to make available for public inspection "all records concerning the implementation of programs and activities conducted for the purpose of ensuring the accuracy and currency of official lists of eligible voters." As of November 2026 the federal appeals courts are **split** on whether that covers the statewide voter list itself. The **First Circuit (Maine, 2024) and Tenth Circuit (New Mexico, 2025)** say yes, and say states **may redact highly sensitive fields (SSN, full date of birth) but may not ban downstream use or internet publication**. The **Ninth Circuit (Hawaii, April 2026)** says no: the list is the *object* of list maintenance, not a *record of* it. A Supreme Court petition (No. 26-98) is pending with the state's response due 25 November 2026.

### Cited Findings

**Statutory text and structure**
- Section 20507(i)(1) requires each state, on request, to disclose records "concerning the implementation of programs and activities conducted for the purpose of ensuring the accuracy and currency of official lists of eligible voters," except records relating "to a declination to register to vote or to the identity of a voter registration agency through which any particular voter is registered." Section 20507(i)(2) says covered records "shall include lists of the names and addresses of all persons to whom notices [of removal] are sent, and information concerning whether or not each such person has responded." The Ninth Circuit quotes and analyses both paragraphs. — [PILF v. Nago, 9th Cir. No. 24-6629 (28 Apr 2026), slip op. pp. 2–3, 32–33](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- The NVRA does not apply at all in six states that had no voter registration, or had election-day registration, since before 1 August 1994; Minnesota is one of them. The Eighth Circuit (around 1 September 2026) held that PILF lacked standing to challenge Minnesota's exemption. PILF's similar challenge to Wisconsin's exemption was dismissed and appealed to the Seventh Circuit, and I found no outcome. (Search summary only; the article returned HTTP 403.) — [Minnesota Lawyer, 1 Sep 2026](https://minnlawyer.com/2026/09/01/8th-circuit-tosses-challenge-to-minnesota-voter-act-exemption/); [Democracy Docket, Wisconsin case page](https://www.democracydocket.com/cases/wisconsin-voter-roll-access-challenge/)

**Fourth Circuit: Project Vote/Voting for America v. Long, 682 F.3d 331 (4th Cir. 15 June 2012)**
- The court held that completed voter registration applications, including rejected ones, are records covered by § 8(i)(1). The applications were to be disclosed with Social Security numbers redacted, because SSNs are "uniquely sensitive and vulnerable to abuse." — [Justia Election Law summary, 15 Jun 2012](https://electionlawopinions.justia.com/2012/06/15/project-vote-v-long/); [Civil Rights Litigation Clearinghouse case page](https://clearinghouse.net/case/12202/)
- Policy language often quoted from Long: "Public disclosure promotes transparency in the voting process, and courts should be loath to reject a legislative effort so germane to the integrity of federal elections." Privacy concerns are "not unfounded" but "must be balanced against the many benefits of public disclosure," since "disclosure will assist the identification of both error and fraud," and "[w]ithout such transparency, public confidence in the essential workings of democracy will suffer." (Quoted in the Tenth Circuit's Torrez opinion at 23–24 & n.15.) — [Voter Reference Found. v. Torrez, 10th Cir. (25 Nov 2025)](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- The Ninth Circuit reads Long narrowly. Applications are "direct evidence of the implementation of registration procedures," but Long "did not hold that entire statewide voter lists fall within section 20507(i)." — [Nago slip op. 35–36](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- In 2021 the Fourth Circuit added a limit: "the [NVRA's] disclosure provision does not encompass any relevant record from any source whatsoever, but must be read in conjunction with the various statutes enacted by Congress to protect the privacy of individuals and confidential information held by certain governmental agencies." PILF v. N.C. State Bd. of Elections, 996 F.3d 257, 264 (4th Cir. 2021), quoted in Nago n.13. — [Nago slip op. 36 n.13](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)

**First Circuit: Public Interest Legal Foundation v. Bellows, 92 F.4th 36 (1st Cir. 2 Feb 2024), Maine voter file**
- PILF asked the Maine Secretary of State for the "Party/Campaign Use Voter File." The First Circuit affirmed that § 8(i)(1) covers the Voter File, and that Maine's restrictions on use and publication of the file, and the fines enforcing them, are preempted. — [Justia, No. 23-1361 (1st Cir. 2024)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca1/23-1361/23-1361-2024-02-02.html)
- The court's reasoning was that voter lists record "the end result of [list maintenance] activities," so they "concern[]" the "implementation" of those activities (Bellows at 47, as characterised by the Ninth Circuit). — [Nago slip op. 34](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- Bellows on redaction: "nothing in the text of the NVRA prohibits the appropriate redaction of uniquely or highly sensitive personal information in the Voter File" (Bellows at 56). On dissemination: "the analysis and subsequent dissemination of Voter File data to the public is necessary if members of the public, or organizations such as [PILF], are ever to identify, address, and fix irregularities in States' voter rolls" (Bellows at 54). Both passages are quoted in Torrez. — [Torrez, 10th Cir. slip op. 22–23 & n.14](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- EPIC filed an amicus brief supporting Maine's voter-privacy law in this appeal. — [EPIC](https://epic.org/?p=33423)

**Tenth Circuit: Voter Reference Foundation v. Torrez, 160 F.4th 1068 (10th Cir. 25 Nov 2025), New Mexico and VoteRef.com**
- Voter Reference Foundation (VRF) runs VoteRef.com. The site generally publishes each voter's "name, birth year, registration address, registration date, party affiliation, registration status, precinct, and voting participation history," refreshed quarterly. — [Torrez slip op. 3–4](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- After VRF posted New Mexico data, the Secretary of State's office said it was "misleading the public" about the 2020 election and referred the matter for criminal prosecution. New Mexico later codified a "Data Sharing Ban" on internet publication, N.M. Stat. § 1-4-5.6(A)(2). — [Torrez slip op. 5–8, 24–25](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- Holding: New Mexico's "Use Restrictions" (voter data may be used only for governmental, election, or campaign purposes) and its internet-publication ban are conflict-preempted by the NVRA. The panel did not reach the First Amendment claims. — [Torrez slip op. 2, 21–25](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- Key passage: "by prohibiting certain uses of New Mexico's voter data, the Use Restrictions obstruct the NVRA's primary goal of providing broad transparency and circulation of such voter data." The NVRA does not limit disclosure to state-to-requester; "the statute encourages broad and extensive public disclosure of relevant records without restrictions on who provides it." — [Torrez slip op. 22, 24](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- On privacy: the court rejected New Mexico's argument that privacy protection to encourage registration should outrank fraud detection. It said "Congress did not rank the importance of the enumerated purposes" and that balancing is for legislatures. — [Torrez slip op. 23](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- On redaction, footnote 14: "To the extent the State wishes to redact appropriate personal information before providing the voter data, the NVRA does not prohibit that limitation." It cites New Mexico's bar on releasing SSNs, agency codes, day and month of birth, and phone numbers. "The State may limit such sensitive information, but ... restricting uses and/or publication altogether is prohibited by the NVRA." The court also said "there may be avenues in which New Mexico may restrict selling or loaning data without disrupting the purposes of the NVRA, [but] it is a step too far to prohibit public access altogether." — [Torrez slip op. 23 n.14, 25](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- The district court ruling that VRF could publish did not extend to voters enrolled in New Mexico's confidential-address program for domestic-violence and stalking victims (secondary report). — [EPIC / search summary of VoteRef coverage](https://epic.org/?p=47413)

**Ninth Circuit: PILF v. Nago, No. 24-6629, 174 F.4th 664 (9th Cir. 28 Apr 2026), Hawaii**
- Hawaii's State Elections Office refused to give PILF a statewide list and told it to ask Hawaii's four county clerks for county lists instead. The panel (McKeown, Friedland, Sung; opinion by Friedland) found standing and ripeness, then held on the merits that "a statewide voter list is not a 'record[] concerning the implementation of programs and activities' ... Covered 'records' are those about the implementation of efforts to ensure the accuracy of voter lists, not voter lists themselves." The claim was dismissed with prejudice. — [Nago slip op. 1–3, 37](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- The panel expressly disagreed with Bellows. The First Circuit's interpretation "collapses the textual distinction between administrative records reflecting implementation of list-maintenance activities and the lists those programs maintain." The panel noted that Torrez "did not address" this textual argument. — [Nago slip op. 34–35 & n.11](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- HAVA, not the NVRA, governs statewide lists: "The Help America Vote Act does not have a public disclosure provision, nor does it create a private right of action ... We decline to read the NVRA to provide a right for the public to access statewide voter lists when Congress chose not to establish that right in the statute specifically addressing the creation of statewide voter lists." — [Nago slip op. 33–34](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- On oversight and privacy: "Effective oversight does not require disclosure of every registrant's personal information; it requires transparency about how list-maintenance programs are implemented." Also: "it is unlikely that Congress would have required states to publicly disclose information that could include every voter's name, address, partial social security numbers, and voting history—information that could be used for harassment, intimidation, or commercial exploitation—without so much as a word about redaction, security, or permitted use." — [Nago slip op. 36–37](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- The panel also rejected district-court rulings for PILF: Torrez (D.N.M. 2024), PILF v. Knapp (D.S.C. 2024) and PILF v. Matthews (C.D. Ill. 2022). — [Nago slip op. 35 n.12](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)

**Supreme Court petition, PILF v. Nago, No. 26-98 (pending)**
- Docket history: petition filed 17 Jul 2026; Hawaii waived response 13 Aug; distributed for the 28 Sep conference; amicus briefs filed 21 Aug by Honest Elections Project, Center for Election Confidence and Judicial Watch; Court requested a response 26 Aug; deadline extended twice, now **25 Nov 2026**. No grant or denial as of this research. — [Supreme Court docket No. 26-98](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/26-98.html)
- The amici describe a circuit conflict (1st and 10th v. 9th). — [Honest Elections Project amicus brief, 21 Aug 2026](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26-98/419794/20260821142331044_PILF%20v.%20Nago%20amicus%20brief%20No.%2026-98.pdf)

**Eleventh Circuit: Greater Birmingham Ministries v. Secretary of State of Alabama, 105 F.4th 1324 (11th Cir. 26 Jun 2024)**
- The court held that felony-disqualification removal records are covered by § 8(i). It reversed the district court on format: the NVRA requires public inspection and photocopying, **not electronic production**, so a state may charge state-law fees for electronic copies. (Secondary summaries; opinion not opened.) — [hallapproved.com case record](https://hallapproved.com/us/cases/ca11/2024/9986692/); [Cumberland Law Review summary](https://cumberlandlawreview.com/post/eleventh-circuit-reverses-district-court-s-injunction-while-reaffirming-longstanding-statutory-inter); [Democracy Docket case page](https://www.democracydocket.com/cases/alabama-voter-purge-nvra-challenge/)

**Third Circuit: standing limits. PILF v. Secretary of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, 136 F.4th 456 (3d Cir. 2025)**
- PILF sought records on registrants flagged as potential noncitizens after a Pennsylvania DMV software glitch. The Third Circuit held PILF lacked standing. Under TransUnion, a bare denial of information is not enough without "a nexus among any adverse effect or downstream consequence and a harm it has suffered." Later filings report cert. denied, No. 25-379 (2 Mar 2026). (Secondary sources; docket not opened.) — [Pennsylvania Capital-Star](https://penncapital-star.com/justice-the-courts/out-of-state-group-cant-sue-for-pa-voter-registration-records-federal-appeals-court-rules/); [Casemine commentary](https://www.casemine.com/commentary/us/transunion-standard-applied-to-nvra:-informational-injury-requires-concrete-downstream-harm/view); [SCOTUS docket 25-379](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-379.html)
- This conflicts with the Ninth Circuit's Nago holding that "the bare denial of a request for information under the NVRA causes an injury in fact sufficient to support Article III standing." — [Nago slip op. 2](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)

**Third Circuit: internet-publication ban. Voter Reference Foundation v. Schmidt (pending, 3d Cir. No. 26-2280)**
- Pennsylvania told VRF in a January letter (year not given in the excerpt I saw) that state law bars publishing voter rolls online, and VoteRef removed the Pennsylvania data. The M.D. Pa. (Judge Saporito) dismissed VRF's NVRA and First Amendment suit in April 2026. EPIC reports the court treated the ban as a limit on access to government-held information, not a speech restriction. VRF's appeal was briefed in August–September 2026, and EPIC filed an amicus brief on 23 Sep 2026 supporting the ban. No decision found. — [Penn Capital-Star, Apr 2026](https://penncapital-star.com/?p=66250); [EPIC, Sep 2026](https://epic.org/epic-urges-third-circuit-to-uphold-pennsylvanias-protections-against-posting-voter-data-online/); [district court opinion (EPIC copy)](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/VRF-v.-Schmidt-district-court-op.pdf)

**Judicial Watch cases (district level)**
- Judicial Watch v. Lamone, 399 F. Supp. 3d 425 (D. Md. 8 Aug 2019): a voter list is a covered record because "a voter list is simply a pared down compilation of voter registrations." Maryland's rule limiting list access to Maryland registered voters was held preempted. Judicial Watch received a Montgomery County list "that includes fields indicating names, home address, most recent voter activity, and active or inactive status." Dates of birth were addressed in a 2020 follow-up ("Lamone II," 455 F. Supp. 3d 209 (D. Md. 17 Apr 2020)), which reportedly allowed access to birthdates. — [Justia, Doc. 54 (2019)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/maryland/mddce/1:2017cv02006/395134/54/); [Clearinghouse](https://clearinghouse.net/case/44881/); [Law360 headline "Conservative Group Can Access Birthdates On Md. Voter Rolls"](https://www.law360.com/articles/1265071)

### Inferences
- **Does § 8(i) oblige states to release the full list?** It depends on the circuit. In the 1st and 10th Circuits (and in Maryland under Lamone) the answer is yes, with sensitive fields redactable. In the 9th Circuit the answer is no, at least for a statewide compiled list. The question is now before the Supreme Court (cert. pending). None of these cases requires release of SSNs or driver's licence numbers; every court that addressed it allows redaction of "uniquely sensitive" fields.
- **Can states restrict uses?** Where § 8(i) covers the list (1st and 10th Circuits), a state cannot ban internet republication or limit use to election and campaign purposes. Torrez leaves room to restrict *selling or loaning*. Where § 8(i) does not cover the list (9th Circuit), state-law use restrictions are not preempted. The M.D. Pa. ruling in VRF v. Schmidt (on appeal) upholds an online-publication ban.
- For Korea, the US litigation shows a structural point. Once a statute frames list access as a *transparency right for oversight of list maintenance*, US courts have read it to forbid conditions on downstream use. A Korean design that wants transparency **and** use limits would need to spell out the limits in the same statute, rather than rely on a separate privacy law. Congress did not do this, and the Ninth Circuit pointed to that silence on "redaction, security, or permitted use."

## Q2. HAVA statewide databases, the Driver's Privacy Protection Act, and federal limits on SSN and driver's licence data

### Takeaway
HAVA (2002) requires each state to keep a single, centralized, computerized statewide list. Applicants must give a driver's licence number or the last four digits of their SSN, and the state must secure the list against unauthorized access. **HAVA contains no public-disclosure provision.** Federal privacy law (the Privacy Act, the DPPA, Social Security Act confidentiality rules, and the constitutional holding in *Greidinger v. Davis*) is the reason states redact SSN and DL fields, and that redaction is what triggered the 2025–2026 federal lawsuits.

### Cited Findings
- **HAVA § 303, 52 U.S.C. § 21083(a)(1)(A)** requires "a single, uniform, official, centralized, interactive computerized statewide voter registration list." Under § 21083(a)(5)(A), an application may not be accepted unless it includes a current driver's licence number or, if none, "the last 4 digits of the applicant's social security number"; applicants with neither get a state-assigned number. Section 21083(a)(3) says officials "shall provide adequate technological security measures to prevent the unauthorized access to the computerized list." Section 21083(a)(5)(B) requires the DMV to match data with the Social Security Administration under 42 U.S.C. § 405(r). The section has no public-disclosure clause. — [52 U.S.C. § 21083, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/52/21083)
- The Ninth Circuit relied on this absence: HAVA "does not have a public disclosure provision, nor does it create a private right of action." — [Nago slip op. 33–34](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- In United States v. Benson, the W.D. Mich. court (10 Feb 2026) held that HAVA has no disclosure provision for the statewide list. It also held that the NVRA's disclosure provisions do not require disclosure of the sensitive fields DOJ sought: full name, DOB, address, DL number, partial SSN. — [CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11456, 14 Jul 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-07-14_LSB11456_fee98cc1ccd8425cf762d905e263b980da3222ed.html)
- **Constitutional limit on SSN publication: Greidinger v. Davis, 988 F.2d 1344 (4th Cir. 1993).** Virginia's practice of making voters' SSNs publicly available as a condition of registering "creates an intolerable burden on [the] right" to vote. The court noted identity-theft exposure that is "alarming and potentially financially ruinous." Virginia could stop collecting SSNs or stop disclosing them publicly. — [EPIC archive of Greidinger opinion](https://archive.epic.org/privacy/ssn/greidinger.html); [Studicata summary](https://www.studicata.com/summaries/united-states-court-of-appeals-fourth-circuit/greidinger-v-davis-1993-t65dyn)
- Some state laws bar disclosure of SSNs, DMV numbers and full date of birth. State officials cite these laws, together with parts of federal law, as the reason they cannot give out entire lists. — [NCSL, Federal Requests for Statewide Voter Lists](https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/federal-requests-for-statewide-voter-lists)
- **DPPA, 18 U.S.C. § 2721.** A state DMV "shall not knowingly disclose or otherwise make available ... personal information ... obtained by the department in connection with a motor vehicle record." There is an exception "[f]or use by any government agency, including any ... law enforcement agency, in carrying out its functions" (§ 2721(b)(1)). DOJ's Office of Legal Counsel concluded on 12 May 2026 that the DPPA does not stop DOJ from obtaining statewide lists, because of the government-function exception. OLC reached the same conclusion for the Privacy Act and the E-Government Act. — [OLC, "Authority to Obtain and Share Statewide Voter Roll Data," 50 Op. O.L.C. __ (12 May 2026), pp. 29–31](https://www.justice.gov/olc/media/1440346/dl)
- EPIC summarizes the DPPA's permissible uses. In 2023 a party argued to the Third Circuit that the DPPA covers only "motor vehicle records" and does not override NVRA disclosure of voter-registration records. That is advocacy, not a holding. — [EPIC DPPA explainer](https://epic.org/dppa/); [3d Cir. filing, 13 Nov 2023](https://democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/3d-Cir.-23-01590-dckt-000044_000-filed-2023-11-13.pdf)
- **The Privacy Act was held to limit federal use of the lists.** On 9 Oct 2026, D.D.C. Judge Sparkle Sooknanan held that DOJ's "Voter List Maintenance Policy" violates "both substantive and procedural protections in the Privacy Act, a statute designed to ban federal data banks." The court also found violations of the Paperwork Reduction Act and APA arbitrary-and-capricious review, and found no authority for the policy in the Civil Rights Act of 1960, the NVRA or HAVA. — [Common Cause v. DOJ, No. 26-cv-1352 (D.D.C. 9 Oct 2026), mem. op. 2–3](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)
- The NVRA itself protects one category: § 20507(i)(1) bars disclosure of the agency through which a voter registered, and of declinations to register. The Ninth Circuit read this as Congress "recogniz[ing] the need to protect the privacy of personal information even while mandating oversight of maintenance procedures." — [Nago slip op. 32–33, 36–37](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)

### Inferences
- The US federal system has no single privacy rule for the voter list. Privacy comes from (a) state statutes, (b) the HAVA security clause (which governs unauthorized access, not public release), (c) Greidinger's constitutional bar on publishing SSNs, in the Fourth Circuit only, and (d) the Privacy Act, which binds *federal* agencies and not states. The NVRA's transparency mandate is express while these privacy limits are scattered, and courts in the 1st and 10th Circuits have tipped toward disclosure.
- In US practice, the fields that separate a "public" list from a "full" list are: SSN or SSN4, DL number, full DOB, phone, email, and the registration agency. States typically release name, address, party, birth year (sometimes full DOB) and vote history.

### Gaps
- I did not find the text of the Social Security Act confidentiality provision (42 U.S.C. § 405(c)(2)(C)(viii)) applied to voter lists in any 2025–2026 ruling, or any appellate holding squarely deciding whether DMV-derived fields in a voter list fall under the DPPA.

## Q3. The 2025–2026 DOJ demands for full voter rolls: what happened, state responses, lawsuits, and status as of November 2026

### Takeaway
From May 2025, DOJ's Civil Rights Division demanded unredacted statewide lists (DOB, address, DL number or SSN4) from 49 states and DC. It shared the data it received with DHS, which ran it through the SAVE citizenship database, and arranged to give it to ICE's Homeland Security Investigations. States receiving DOJ flags agreed to "clean" them within 45 days. **16 to 18 states complied fully. DOJ sued 30 states and DC and, as of 10–12 November 2026, had lost 28 cases.** Oklahoma settled; Hawaii and Idaho are pending. The **Sixth Circuit affirmed** the Michigan loss and denied rehearing en banc. On 9 October 2026 a D.D.C. court **vacated the entire DOJ policy nationwide**, including for states that cooperated. DOJ has appealed to the D.C. Circuit.

### Cited Findings

**What DOJ asked for and why**
- In May 2025 the Civil Rights Division began requesting "complete and unredacted copies" of statewide voter registration lists, "including voters' full names, dates of birth, residential addresses, and state driver's license numbers or last four digits of Social Security numbers." Requests went to 49 states and DC; North Dakota has no registration list. DOJ assured states the data would be "treated consistently with the Privacy Act." The project implemented Executive Order 14248 (2025). — [Common Cause v. DOJ mem. op. 8–9 & n.2 (D.D.C. 9 Oct 2026)](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)
- DOJ's stated purpose was to check compliance with the list-maintenance requirements of HAVA and the NVRA. — [CBS News, updated 10 Nov 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-lawsuits-state-voter-rolls-justice-department/)
- OLC informally advised in early September 2025, and formally on 12 May 2026, that the Civil Rights Act of 1960 authorizes the demands. It also advised that DOJ may share the lists with DHS and HSI "for the purpose of identifying illegal aliens who are ineligible to vote," and that the Privacy Act, DPPA and E-Government Act do not prevent this. — [OLC opinion, 12 May 2026](https://www.justice.gov/olc/media/1440346/dl)

**What DOJ did with the data (findings from the administrative record)**
- DOJ shared unredacted lists with DHS, which ran them through SAVE. SAVE was modified in 2025 to include SSA records for the first time. — [Common Cause mem. op. 10–11](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)
- DOJ's own information-sharing memo acknowledged that "SAVE may only be able to verify acquired U.S. citizens in certain situations." SSA did not consistently record citizenship until 1981 and does not update it after naturalization. A 2006 SSA audit found that 7% of SSA's noncitizen records, about 3.3 million people, misidentified US citizens as noncitizens. — [Common Cause mem. op. 11–12](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)
- DOJ agreed to give state lists to Homeland Security Investigations (within ICE) so HSI could "conduct appropriate investigation of potential violations of federal election law" and "enrich the SVRL datasets with DHS records." MOUs with Alaska, South Carolina and Texas allowed disclosure to DOJ contractors. States agreed to "clean" their lists within 45 days of a DOJ notice by removing voters DOJ deemed ineligible. — [Common Cause mem. op. 12–13](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)
- At a March 2026 hearing in the Rhode Island case, a DOJ official said the voter data would be run "against DHS'S SAVE ... database." Internal correspondence from summer 2026 reportedly showed DOJ had disclosed records to DHS, which used them with Palantir's ELITE application for immigration enforcement. That last point is reported, and I did not verify it. — [Brennan Center tracker, updated 10 Nov 2026](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/tracker-justice-department-requests-voter-information)

**State responses**
- At least 16 states gave complete lists with DL and SSN data: Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, Florida, Indiana, Iowa, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nebraska, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas and Wyoming. **Other federal demands**
- Colorado and Missouri refused DOJ requests to access voting equipment. The FBI obtained 2020 election records from Fulton County, GA and Maricopa County, AZ by warrant or subpoena. — [Brennan Center tracker](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/tracker-justice-department-requests-voter-information)

### Inferences
- The courts' reasoning so far rests on *statutory authority* (Title III's "come into possession" and "basis and purpose" requirements, the Privacy Act, the PRA). It does not rest on a constitutional privacy right, so Congress could change the outcome by statute. The Supreme Court's 25 Sep 2026 stay in the SAVE case shows the high court may be more receptive to the federal government than the lower courts.
- The episode is a concrete example of a risk Korea should model: a full list with identifiers, once held centrally, can be **repurposed for immigration enforcement and automated purges**. The harm came less from "public" access than from aggregation and matching against error-prone databases (see the SSA 7% figure and the Nel example in Q5).

### Gaps
- Not found: a D.C. Circuit ruling on the stay; the status of appeals in the other circuits (1st, 3rd, 4th, 9th, 10th and others) from the 28 dismissals; Hawaii and Idaho rulings; aggregate numbers of voters flagged or removed through SAVE under the DOJ program.

## Q4. Documented benefits of public voter lists (research, journalism, list-maintenance audits, citizen auditing, campaign outreach)

### Takeaway
The strongest documented benefits are **research infrastructure** (validated-turnout studies, field experiments, the double-voting estimate itself), **journalistic accountability** (exposing officials' improper registrations), and **independent measurement of list quality** (Pew 2012). Evidence that *citizen* auditing of public lists has found real ineligibility at scale is thin. Most mass challenges fail (see Q5). The courts' transparency rationale (Long, Bellows, Torrez) is normative rather than empirical.

### Cited Findings
- **List-quality measurement.** Pew's *Inaccurate, Costly, and Inefficient* (14 Feb 2012), conducted with RTI International, estimated that about 24 million registrations (1 in 8) were invalid or significantly inaccurate. That included more than 1.8 million deceased people still listed, about 2.75 million people registered in more than one state, and about 12 million records with incorrect addresses. About 51 million eligible citizens were unregistered. Pew's David Becker said the study "found no evidence that any of the inaccuracies led to voter fraud or voter suppression." — [Pew report page](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2012/02/14/inaccurate-costly-and-inefficient); [Pew press release](https://www.pew.org/en/about/news-room/press-releases/2012/02/14/pew-one-in-eight-voter-registrations-inaccurate-51-million-citizens-unregistered); [Becker interview](https://www.pewtrusts.org/research-and-analysis/articles/2012/02/28/david-becker-upgrading-voter-registration)
- **Academic validation research.** Ansolabehere and Hersh, "Validation: What Big Data Reveal About Survey Misreporting and the Real Electorate," *Political Analysis* 20(4): 437–459 (2012), matched a 50-state survey (2008 CCES) to every state's voter file through a commercial vendor. They found that politically engaged nonvoters systematically over-report voting, so survey-only studies "dramatically misestimate the differences between voters and nonvoters." — [Yale ISPS publication page](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps12-021); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:polals:v:20:y:2012:i:04:p:437-459_01)
- **Field experiments on turnout.** Gerber, Green and Larimer, *APSR* 102(1): 33–48 (2008), sent mailings to about 180,000 Michigan households before the August 2006 primary, using public vote-history records. The "neighbors" mailing raised turnout by about 8 percentage points. — [Yale ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps08-001); [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/social-pressure-and-voter-turnout-united-states)
- **Fraud measurement.** Goel et al., "One Person, One Vote," *APSR* 114(2): 456–469 (2020), used national voter files to estimate that about 1 in 4,000 voters cast two ballots in 2012. An audit suggested the true rate is lower because of clerical errors in electronic vote records. — [Cambridge/APSR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/one-person-one-vote-estimating-the-prevalence-of-double-voting-in-us-presidential-elections/F0F11207B6EC1A0A5DE18DC283ACE926/share/f196dcf91a6c8aa31c5793ad900a47aa727975bb); [working paper PDF](https://www.5harad.com/papers/1p1v.pdf)
- **Journalism.** Public voter records showed that former White House chief of staff Mark Meadows had registered in North Carolina at a mobile home he did not own, weeks before voting in 2020. The New Yorker first reported this; the Washington Post Fact Checker then found from state records that he was registered in three states at once (NC, VA, SC). This led to a State Bureau of Investigation probe, and Macon County removed him from the rolls in April 2022. — [WUNC, 22 Apr 2022](https://www.wunc.org/news/2022-04-22/trumps-former-chief-of-staff-mark-meadows-was-registered-to-vote-in-three-states-officials-say); [WFAE, 13 Apr 2022](https://www.wfae.org/politics/2022-04-13/a-north-carolina-county-has-removed-mark-meadows-as-a-registered-voter)
- In January 2017 journalists reported, from voter records, that White House adviser Steve Bannon was registered in two states, including at a vacant Florida house he did not live in. — [The Week, Jan 2017](https://theweek.com/speedreads/675704/president-trumps-advisers-reportedly-registered-vote-two-states)
- **Citizen auditing and challenges.** In Denton County, Texas (2024), the elections administrator said activists' challenges (some made with True the Vote's IV3 app) can speed up the residence-verification process the county already runs. The same reporting notes errors, including challenges to assisted-living residents. — [CNN via Yahoo, 2024](https://news.yahoo.com/news/half-million-records-one-app-090002974.html)
- **Judicial statements of the oversight rationale.** Long (2012): disclosure "will assist the identification of both error and fraud." Bellows (2024): dissemination is "necessary" for the public "to identify, address, and fix irregularities in States' voter rolls." Torrez (2025): "widely circulating the voter data is so that any inaccuracies can be identified and corrected." — [Torrez slip op. 22–24](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- **Counter-view (Ninth Circuit, 2026).** "Effective oversight does not require disclosure of every registrant's personal information; it requires transparency about how list-maintenance programs are implemented." — [Nago slip op. 36](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- **Campaign outreach.** Maine's file at issue in Bellows was the "Party/Campaign Use Voter File." New Mexico's restricted uses included "election-campaign purposes." Officials quoted in reporting on voter-shaming mailers said voter files are "mainly used for get-out-the-vote work." — [Justia (Bellows)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca1/23-1361/23-1361-2024-02-02.html); [Torrez slip op. 21](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf); [CBS Chicago, voter-shaming letters](https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/some-voters-are-getting-shaming-letters/)

### Inferences
- Most of the high-value research uses (Ansolabehere–Hersh, Goel et al., Pew/RTI) depended on **national commercial voter files**, which vendors build by aggregating state lists. These benefits require research access, not necessarily open internet publication. A gated researcher, journalist and party access tier (like many US states' "qualified requester" models) could keep most of the benefit.
- The journalistic examples needed only name, address and registration history, not identifiers.
- The research itself undercuts the main argument of citizen auditors: Pew found no evidence of fraud from inaccuracies, and Goel et al. found double voting to be very rare.

### Gaps
- I found no systematic study measuring how many ineligible registrations were removed *because of* citizen or NGO audits of public lists, as opposed to official sources such as ERIC, NCOA or SSA death data.
- I found no quantified study of campaign reliance on public lists (for example, share of contact or turnout effects attributable to voter-file access).
- PILF's own reports on deceased registrants were not reviewed.

## Q5. Documented harms: mass challenges, false positives, intimidation canvasses, doxxing and republication, breaches, vulnerable voters, chilling effects

### Takeaway
The best-documented harms are: (1) **mass eligibility challenges** built from public lists plus crude matching (Georgia: 364,000 challenges in December 2020 and at least 63,000 in 2024, with few upheld); (2) **systematic false positives** from name and birthdate matching (Crosscheck-style purges would remove about 300 legitimate voters per double vote prevented) and from citizenship databases (a citizen's registration cancelled through SAVE in 2025–2026); (3) **intimidating door-to-door canvasses** (Colorado USEIP; retrial ordered July 2026); (4) **internet republication** (VoteRef, covering tens of millions of voters with vote history); and (5) **large exposures of aggregated voter files** (191 million records in 2015, 198 million in 2017). Evidence of **chilling effects** comes from one natural experiment: after the 2017 Kobach commission request, more than 3,000 Colorado voters withdrew their registrations. Systematic deterrence studies were not found.

### Cited Findings

**Mass challenges (Georgia; True the Vote; EagleAI; IV3)**
- In December 2020, True the Vote and others challenged more than 364,000 Georgia voters across all 159 counties; most counties rejected these challenges. In fall 2022 challenges covered about 65,000 registrations in at least 8 counties (VoterGA alone filed 37,500 in Gwinnett). At least 1,800 voters were removed in 8 counties as of 29 July 2022. — [Brennan Center, "Mass Voter Challenges and Voter List Maintenance in Georgia," 21 Dec 2020, updated 18 Jun 2024](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/mass-voter-challenges-and-voter-list-maintenance-georgia)
- An AP survey of 45 Georgia counties found that Republican activists challenged more than 63,000 voters before the 2024 election, but few were removed. The same survey counted about 18,000 challenges in 2023 and the first half of 2024. (Search summary of the AP and Fox 5 report.) — [Fox 5 Atlanta / AP](https://www.fox5atlanta.com/news/republicans-challenge-georgia-voters-removal-rolls)
- SB 202 (2021) confirmed that a Georgia citizen may challenge an unlimited number of voters in their county. SB 189 (2024, effective July 2024) set new grounds for upholding challenges, such as registering in another state or registering at a nonresidential address. (Search summary.) — [AJC on SB 189](https://ajc.com/politics/georgia-legislature-votes-to-strengthen-ability-to-challenge-voters-eligibility/K2ISGWVA5BF2ZCKFINRN72OGSQ); [Brennan Center analysis](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/new-georgia-law-spurs-bogus-challenges-voter-eligibility)
- **Fair Fight v. True the Vote (N.D. Ga., Judge Steve Jones, 2 Jan 2024).** After a bench trial, the court found no violation of VRA § 11(b) (intimidation). It noted that county boards make the eligibility decision, and said the ruling should not be read as condoning "a mass number of seemingly frivolous challenges." — [Justia, Doc. 335](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/georgia/gandce/2:2020cv00302/285098/335/); [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-sides-with-conservative-group-in-georgia-voter-intimidation-case/)
- EagleAI "compiles data from multiple publicly available sources, including secretary of states' offices and U.S. Postal Service change of address data." The Forsyth County board denied EagleAI-based challenges in April 2024 for lack of evidence beyond EagleAI's data. Critics say voters may have moved back. (Search summary of NPR, 4 Jun 2024.) The Brennan Center describes EagleAI as "simple list matching" on public voter information. — [NPR, 4 Jun 2024](https://www.npr.org/2024/06/04/nx-s1-4991945/voter-registration-mass-challenges-georgia); [Brennan Center Georgia report](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/mass-voter-challenges-and-voter-list-maintenance-georgia)
- IV3 is True the Vote's challenge app. It matches state voter lists against National Change of Address records and has been used in California, Georgia, New York and Texas. In Denton County, TX, about a dozen people filed challenges, sometimes more than 1,000 a day. Texas officials in Brazos, Tarrant and Hays counties said the flood of challenges complicated routine list maintenance. — [Brennan Center, "Four New Initiatives Driving Mass Voter Challenges"](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/four-new-initiatives-driving-mass-voter-challenges); [Votebeat Texas, 15 Aug 2024](https://www.votebeat.org/texas/2024/08/15/true-the-vote-challenges-county-voter-registrations-brazos-tarrant-hays); [CNN via Yahoo](https://news.yahoo.com/news/half-million-records-one-app-090002974.html)
- Demos and All Voting Is Local (September 2024) concluded that the available information on IV3's and EagleAI's methods "suggests they are fundamentally flawed." — [Demos policy brief](https://www.demos.org/policy-briefs/understanding-flawed-data-methodologies-underlying-mass-voter-challenges); [All Voting Is Local white paper, Sep 2024](https://allvotingislocal.org/wp-content/uploads/All-Voting-Report-VPT-White-Paper-SEPT-2024-R3.pdf)
- In September 2024 DOJ issued a 7-page guidance memo saying "systematic" removals, including challenges compiled with programs like EagleAI, fall under the NVRA's 90-day pre-election restriction. (Search summary of AJC reporting; memo not opened.) — [AJC, 2024](https://ajc.com/politics/wave-of-georgia-voter-challenges-expected-before-2024-election/V7OXHYVD7NEZBI76VLGV35OP7M)

**False positives from list matching**
- Goel et al. (*APSR* 2020): the strategy of keeping only the later registration when two records share the same name and birthdate "could impede approximately 300 legitimate votes for each double vote prevented." The 2017 working paper put the figure at about 200 for Interstate Crosscheck-style purges. A 1.3% clerical error rate would be enough to explain all apparent double votes. — [APSR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/one-person-one-vote-estimating-the-prevalence-of-double-voting-in-us-presidential-elections/F0F11207B6EC1A0A5DE18DC283ACE926/share/f196dcf91a6c8aa31c5793ad900a47aa727975bb); [working paper, Jan 2017](https://www.5harad.com/papers/1p1v.pdf)
- **SAVE and SSA citizenship matching (2025–2026).** Plaintiff Anthony Nel, a derived US citizen, had his Texas voter registration "cancelled after the modified SAVE system failed to confirm his citizenship." The same happened to "Bailey Doe," a naturalized citizen. A 2006 SSA audit found that 7% of SSA's noncitizen records, about 3.3 million people, were actually citizens. — [Common Cause v. DOJ mem. op. 11–12, 34](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)

**Data exposure through matching programs**
- Kansas sent Florida, by unsecured email in 2013, the names and partial SSNs of 945 Kansans flagged by Crosscheck. In September 2017 Florida "inadvertently provided" the last four SSN digits of those 945 people to a member of the public in response to a records request, and offered identity-theft protection. — [WUSF, 23 Jan 2018](https://www.wusf.org/news/2018-01-23/voter-data-for-nearly-1k-kansas-residents-exposed-in-florida); [DataBreaches.net, Florida notice](https://www.databreaches.net/florida-department-of-state-notifies-945-of-breach-involving-the-last-four-digits-of-their-ssn)

**Intimidating door-to-door "voter roll" canvasses**
- In Colorado, the NAACP, the League of Women Voters of Colorado and Mi Familia Vota sued the founders of the U.S. Election Integrity Plan (USEIP) in March 2022 over post-2020 door-to-door canvassing. After a bench trial, the district court found in July 2024 that intimidation had not been proven. **On 6 July 2026 the Tenth Circuit reversed**, saying the earlier dismissal of USEIP itself "had a substantial influence on the outcome of the bench trial," and ordered a new trial. — [Colorado Public Radio, 18 Jul 2024](https://cpr.org/2024/07/18/election-intimidation-useip-canvassing-case-dismissed-by-judge); [Colorado Politics, 6 Jul 2026](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/07/06/10th-circuit-revives-colorado-voter-intimidation-lawsuit-due-to-judges-error/); [LWV press release](https://my.lwv.org/node/72990)

**Doxxing and republication websites (VoteRef)**
- VoteRef publishes name, birth year, address, party, status, precinct and **vote-participation history**. — [Torrez slip op. 3–4](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- 404 Media (7 Nov 2024) reported that a person who voted in America "probably" had their data posted on VoteRef, and found social-media users using VoteRef data to spread voting conspiracies. ProPublica-derived reporting says the site covers more than 85 million people and posted names, birthdates, addresses and voting histories for 2 million Nevada voters. Those figures come via reposts; I did not open the original ProPublica article. — [404 Media](https://404media.co/voted-in-america-this-site-doxed-you); [Slashdot repost](https://politics.slashdot.org/story/24/11/07/233235/voted-in-america-voteref-probably-doxed-you)
- Virginia told VRF to remove Virginia rolls (August 2022). Virginia law lets law-enforcement officers, judges, domestic-violence and stalking survivors and others shield their addresses. — [Virginia Mercury, 4 Aug 2022](https://virginiamercury.com/2022/08/04/conservative-group-threatens-lawsuit-after-being-told-it-cant-publish-virginias-voter-rolls/)

**Breaches and exposures of aggregated voter files**
- In December 2015, researcher Chris Vickery found an unsecured, password-free database of about 191 million US voter records (about 300 GB). It held names, addresses, phone numbers, DOB, party and, per some reports, primary and general vote history. It held no SSNs or DL numbers. The owner was never identified, and it was offline by 28 Dec 2015. — [CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/database-leak-exposes-191m-voter-registration-records/); [FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/database-leak-exposes-191-million-voter-registration-records); [Bangor Daily News/AP, 28 Dec 2015](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2015/12/28/news/database-of-191-million-us-voters-exposed-on-the-internet/)
- In June 2017, UpGuard found that RNC contractor Deep Root Analytics had left about 198 million voter records in a publicly accessible AWS S3 bucket (about 25 TB, 1.1 TB downloadable). The data included DOB, addresses, phone numbers, registration details and *modelled* ethnicity and religion. It was found on 12 Jun and secured on 14 Jun 2017. Deep Root said, "We take full responsibility." — [UpGuard, "The RNC Files"](https://www.upguard.com/breaches/the-rnc-files); [CNN Money, 19 Jun 2017](https://money.cnn.com/2017/06/19/technology/voter-data-leaked-online-gop/index.html)

**Vulnerable voters**
- Domestic-violence advocates warn that public voter records expose survivors' addresses. Address-confidentiality programs exist but do not cover survivors who are not enrolled. Advocates say "digitizing and aggregating data meaningfully changes the privacy context and the risks." — [NNEDV Safety Net, voter registration privacy](https://www.techsafety.org/voter-registration-privacy); [NIWRC, "'Safe at Home' Breached," Oct 2022](https://www.niwrc.org/restoration-magazine/october-2022/safe-home-breached); [KUOW, 9 Oct 2018](https://www.kuow.org/governing/2018-10-09/for-domestic-violence-survivors-voting-can-be-dangerous)
- The Ninth Circuit warned that name, address, partial SSN and voting history "could be used for harassment, intimidation, or commercial exploitation." — [Nago slip op. 37](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)
- Greidinger (4th Cir. 1993) held that publicly disclosing SSNs as a condition of registration is an "intolerable burden" on the right to vote. — [EPIC archive](https://archive.epic.org/privacy/ssn/greidinger.html)

**Chilling effects on registration**
- After the Pence-Kobach Presidential Advisory Commission on Election Integrity requested state voter data in late June 2017, at least 3,394 Colorado voters withdrew their registrations within about two weeks. Another 182 made their records confidential. About 825 withdrew in the week of 3 July and about 2,773 in the week of 10 July. — [HPPR, 19 Jul 2017](https://www.hppr.org/hppr-government-politics/2017-07-19/over-3-000-voters-cancel-registrations-following-trump-election-commissions-request-for-voter-info); [Mediaite](https://www.mediaite.com/online/almost-3400-people-in-colorado-canceled-voter-registration-following-commission-request-for-info/amp)
- Totals conflict. Colorado Politics reported more than 5,000 withdrawals by 1 August 2017, while a later Colorado Independent retrospective gave 3,189. About 88% of withdrawals were by Democrats and unaffiliated voters. Secretary of State Wayne Williams said there was no evidence the withdrawing voters were ineligible. — [Colorado Politics, 1 Aug 2017](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2017/08/01/colorado-secretary-of-state-wayne-williams-turns-over-state-voter-data-to-white-house-commission); [Colorado Independent](https://coloradoindependent.com/168121/trump-voter-fraud-commission-colorado); [Denver7](https://www.denver7.com/news/politics/88-percent-of-colorado-voter-registration-withdrawals-are-from-democrats-unaffiliated-voters); [HPPR, 27 Jul 2017](https://www.hppr.org/hppr-government-politics/2017-07-27/no-evidence-colorado-voters-who-canceled-registrations-were-ineligible)
- Withdrawing voters cited distrust of the commission and *learning how much of their registration data was already public*. — [IVN, 14 Jul 2017](https://ivn.us/2017/07/14/co-election-officials-never-seen-exodus-voters)
- A 2020 William & Mary Law Review note argues that public registration records act as a form of vote denial. It cites anecdotes, such as a Florida official whose constituents asked to be removed from distribution, but no systematic measurement. — [Sauer, "Privacy or the Polls," 61 Wm. & Mary L. Rev. 1473 (2020)](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/wmlr/vol61/iss5/6)
- In the 2026 DOJ litigation, Common Cause showed organizational harm. Its staff had to contact Texas members who "may have been wrongly flagged for removal" and spent dozens of hours responding to voter concerns in Nebraska and Ohio. — [Common Cause mem. op. ~21–22](https://www.democracydocket.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/51-2026-10-09-Memorandum-opinion.pdf)

### Inferences
- The causal chain behind most documented harms is: **public list → bulk download → crude matching (name + DOB, NCOA, citizenship databases) → mass challenge, purge or confrontation.** The false-positive rate of the *matching* step, not publicity alone, drives disenfranchisement. Fields that make matching easy, such as full DOB, also make it misleadingly confident.
- Republication harms (doxxing, survivors' addresses) depend on **address + name + vote history in one searchable place**. US states that allow access but ban internet republication (for example Pennsylvania, upheld at district level in 2026) are attempting a middle path. In the 1st and 10th Circuits that path is foreclosed by the NVRA.
- The 2015 and 2017 breaches show that once lists are released to campaigns and vendors, they are aggregated into national files that hold *more* than any state released (modelled ethnicity and religion), and security then rests with private contractors.
- The Colorado 2017 episode is the clearest US evidence that publicity and centralization *salience* can depress registration. It is short-run and observational.

### Gaps
- Not found: peer-reviewed causal studies measuring whether public voter files (or their online republication) reduce registration or turnout.
- Not found: statewide official totals of 2024–2026 Georgia challenges and removals (the AP survey covers 45 counties).
- I have no documented, specific cases of physical harm to individuals traced to VoteRef or similar sites; reporting describes risk and misuse for conspiracy claims.
- I did not open the NPR EagleAI article (503 error) or the AP 63K article directly; figures are from search summaries.

## Q6. Does publishing participation history (who voted) create coercion or vote-buying risks?

### Takeaway
US evidence shows that public vote history **enables social-pressure tactics**. Field experiments raised turnout by about 8 points by showing neighbours' voting records. Real-world "voter report card" mailers drew complaints and, in Maryland in 2024, an Attorney General cease-and-desist on intimidation grounds. The comparative political economy literature shows that **turnout is the observable that lets machines buy turnout or abstention despite the secret ballot**, so public vote-history records lower the cost of monitoring. I found **no US study documenting vote buying enabled by public vote-history files**.

### Cited Findings
- Gerber, Green and Larimer (*APSR* 2008): of four randomized mailings to about 180,000 Michigan households in 2006, the one listing the household's and neighbours' voting records had the largest effect, about +8 points in turnout. Many recipients of that treatment called to complain. The authors cite "reactance" and "boomerang" effects. — [Yale ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps08-001); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/social-pressure-and-voter-turnout-united-states); [Adam Brown's notes on the study](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/social_pressure_and_voter_turnout)
- Real campaigns have copied the tactic. Mailers listing recipients' and neighbours' vote history went out in Chicago, Texas, Los Angeles, Maine, Oklahoma and other places in 2018–2024. Recipients called them "shaming" and "threatening"; the California Secretary of State received complaints; Club for Growth defended the method as "scientifically proven." Maryland's Attorney General Anthony Brown sent a cease-and-desist to the Center for Voter Information in November 2024, arguing the mailers violated the state law against influencing voters by intimidation. The group's lawyer disputed that any law was violated. — [CBS Chicago](https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/some-voters-are-getting-shaming-letters/); [ABC7 Chicago](https://abc7chicago.com/post/voter-shaming-mailer-sent-to-chicago-voters-claims-to-tell-which-neighbors-have-voted-in-recent-elections/5152212/); [CBS Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/mailer-compares-neighbors-voting-habits); [Washington Times, 1 Nov 2024](https://m.washingtontimes.com/news/2024/nov/1/anthony-brown-demands-liberal-group-stop-sending-t/); [WEAR, "Social pressure or voter intimidation?"](https://weartv.com/news/nation-world/social-pressure-or-voter-intimidation-debate-surrounds-controversial-voting-report-cards-history-2024-election-ohio-oregon-language-mailers-politics)
- One Maine recipient said the mailer wrongly listed her as not voting in 2008, which illustrates accuracy problems in vote-history data. (Search summary.) — [Central Maine](https://www.centralmaine.com/?p=340267)
- **Turnout buying under a secret ballot.** Nichter, "Vote Buying or Turnout Buying? Machine Politics and the Secret Ballot," *APSR* 102(1): 19–31 (2008), argues that parties can reward supporters for showing up, which requires verifying only *whether* someone voted, not *how*. This explains clientelism under secret ballots; Argentine data fit turnout buying. — [Cambridge/APSR](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/vote-buying-or-turnout-buying-machine-politics-and-the-secret-ballot/550A40E8B9C692AC2FB7EBB4676B7EAB); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:apsrev:v:102:y:2008:i:01:p:19-31_08)
- Gans-Morse, Mazzuca and Nichter (*AJPS* 2014) model four strategies: vote buying, turnout buying, *abstention buying* (paying opponents to stay home) and double persuasion. Abstention buying is likewise verifiable through turnout records. — [Gans-Morse et al., AJPS 2014 (PDF)](https://sites.northwestern.edu/jgansmorse/files/2021/03/ajps12058.pdf)
- Ascencio and Chang (*Political Science Research and Methods*, 2024) find experimentally that vote-buying offers make respondents "significantly more likely to doubt ballot secrecy." Brokers can therefore induce compliance by undermining confidence in secrecy, and turnout and abstention buying exploit the same imperfect secrecy. — [PSRM article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/does-vote-buying-undermine-confidence-in-ballot-secrecy-theory-and-experimental-evidence/0B56CDC04C82B89D1BD390AE1DFFD903)
- Aidt and Jensen (*Comparative Political Studies*, 2017) study turnout before and after secret-ballot reform in US states and British constituencies, as evidence on how vote markets declined. — [Cambridge repository, "From Open to Secret Ballot"](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/50a101fe-5673-416a-aa49-63df88116706)
- VoteRef publishes voting-participation history nationwide. — [Torrez slip op. 3–4](https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/sites/ca10/files/opinions/010111342881.pdf)
- The Ninth Circuit listed "voting history" among the data that "could be used for harassment, intimidation, or commercial exploitation." — [Nago slip op. 37](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/04/28/24-6629.pdf)

### Inferences
- Publishing *who voted* does not breach ballot secrecy (*how* someone voted), but it gives brokers, employers, family members and community leaders a cheap, official tool to check compliance with turnout or abstention demands. This risk is greater in tightly knit or clientelist settings, and in small precincts where turnout is low.
- For Korea, the US experience suggests separating three things: (a) the list of eligible electors, (b) individual participation marks, and (c) access tiers. If participation history is released at all, it could go only to candidates and parties for a short, purpose-limited window (or only as precinct aggregates). Open republication of vote history is where US evidence shows social-pressure and shaming uses.

### Gaps
- I found no US empirical study linking public vote-history files to vote buying or employer or family coercion. US evidence is limited to social-pressure mailers and complaints.
- I found no study on whether publishing vote history affects turnout of vulnerable groups, such as survivors or immigrants' family members.
- Not searched: comparative evidence from countries that publish marked registers (for example UK marked registers available to parties). That may be covered by other researchers.
